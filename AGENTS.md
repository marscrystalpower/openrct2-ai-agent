# Agent entry point

You are setting up a community member's installation. Nothing in these files grants permission inherited from another user or a previous development session.

## Explain before installing

Tell the user in plain language: OpenRCT2 is a modern community-built replacement game engine using their own RCT2 graphics and sounds. The bridge needs its plugin support. They will launch OpenRCT2 for agent play; their Steam installation remains available. This bridge sends native game commands rather than mouse clicks. They need their own installed RCT2 assets. Confirm their understanding and resolve missing installation/launch authorization, without asking again if already explicitly authorized in this conversation.

## Own the setup

Read README.md, SETUP.md and VERIFICATION.md. Perform technical setup for the user; do not substitute a command checklist. Check Windows x64, Node >=20, Git and RCT2 assets. If a prerequisite is absent, obtain it from its official publisher using the user's authorized installation scope. Do not silently upgrade an existing runtime or bypass a device policy. Resolve the actual Steam/library or other RCT2 location; never assume a particular Windows username or drive. Validate Data/g1.dat.

Run scripts/Setup-Bridge.ps1. It verifies the exact upstream source commit before building, tests, then installs. Stop on errors. Installation does not imply permission to launch, play, overwrite saves, or publish anything. Launch only when authorized with scripts/Start-Bridge.ps1 -Rct2Path ACTUAL_PATH. Use a new scenario or a copy of a user-selected save. Read-only hello and park inspection must succeed before gameplay. Let the user choose their park and scope/duration. Offer an increased in-game UI scale if controls are too small.

Check furniture along the permitted outbound route. ## Coaster design

- Treat examples/custom-coaster-plan.json as a technical syntax reference, not as a recommended coaster layout or design template.
- Do not precisely copy its station length, lift length, drop length, turn arrangement, straight sections, brake placement, footprint, or overall sequence merely because they appear in the example.
- Before construction, identify several intentional ride moments appropriate to the ride type. These may come from drops, hills, airtime, inversions, helixes, photo sections, banked turns, tunnels, terrain interaction, water features, changes in speed, or other suitable elements.
- Bank curves only when expected speed, radius and tested lateral forces warrant it; use unbanked slow turns at lift crests and after braking. Review runs of four or more ordinary flat straights: shorten them or use suitable hills, turns, helixes or inversions unless they serve a clear purpose. Stations, lifts, launches and braking runs are functional sections.
- On uneven terrain, consider station elevation early in the ride concept. A station near a ridge or plateau can allow a modest initial climb to lead into a much larger descent below station level. This can make the landscape part of the ride’s pacing and identity. Where this approach suits the concept, account for the return to station elevation, support limits, access, and tested speeds and forces throughout the circuit.
- Where it suits the ride’s concept, consider a largely or entirely underground layout, potentially including the station and lift. A concealed route can suggest a journey through caves, mines, pipes, or other enclosed environments while preserving the landscape above. Underground sections can still offer varied drops, hills, turns, and changes in elevation; they need not serve only as short connecting tunnels. This is an optional design approach, not a requirement. If chosen, it calls for attention to terrain cover, clearance, usable entrance and exit paths, and tested ride operation.
- A new coaster should normally be designed as a complete closed circuit unless the ride concept specifically calls for a legitimate non-circuit design such as a shuttle coaster. Do not use an open or incomplete track simply because the example permits it.
- For multiple trains in block mode, plan legal block boundaries and useful braking into block brakes. Ordinary brakes do not create blocks; verify stopping, restart and clearance with the intended train count.
- For substantial circuit coasters, plan block sections and the intended train count before construction, especially rides lasting 90 seconds or more. Review long ordinary brake runs for useful block locations. Verify that the ride supports the mode, that stopped trains can restart, and that the full fleet runs through every section. Do not accept one default train without considering throughput, queues and profit; do not add block brakes to ride types or layouts that cannot use them.
- Default to unbanked turns immediately after a chain lift crest. A long banked section there needs a specific justification from actual crest speed, turn radius and lateral forces. Do not add banking merely because the piece is available or use a long banked detour to fill space.
- Consider giving a ride a recognizable layout motif, such as a repeated sequence of hills, turns, or inversions. Small variations in spacing, elevation, or order can develop that motif without requiring every section to introduce something new. For paired rides, offsetting similar elements may create an interesting visual relationship. These are optional design approaches; any close interaction would still need normal clearance and operating checks.
- Do not begin a new coaster by treating the example blueprint as the starting layout and making small modifications to it. Create a new piece sequence based on the ride concept. Small, compact designs are valid when they suit the concept, audience, budget, or available space; they should not be the automatic choice for construction convenience.
- Begin construction promptly once you have a viable concept; speed of construction should not cause you to substitute simpler geometry for the intended design. Choose the ride's scale and complexity to suit its concept, audience, finances, terrain, and supported elements rather than settling for the smallest technically valid design.
- Test the finished ride with its intended train count. Inspect speed, forces, ratings, reliability and capacity, review a native screenshot, and revise when needed.
- Use `ride`'s `inspection` for native test flags, actual train/car/seat counts, departure waits, music and breakdown state before resorting to save parsing. `null` means unavailable. Object car limits are not the ride's dynamic limits, and the native `tested` flag does not prove that every train completed its run.
- Change the track colours and train colours if the design concept calls for it. Do not change the colour of every ride simply because it is encouraged. Multiple rides may share colours when those colours suit their themes.
- Treat ride appearance and colour as part of the design rather than an afterthought. When the ride type supports recolouring, choose a deliberate colour scheme that complements the ride's name, concept, surrounding scenery, or park theme instead of automatically retaining the default colours.
- Coordinate names and colours when appropriate. A medieval-themed coaster might use a name and colour scheme that reinforce its medieval identity; a jungle ride, futuristic ride, seaside ride, storybook ride, or other themed ride should likewise be allowed to develop its own visual identity.
- Do not use one fixed colour scheme for every coaster. Variety is desirable, but colours should remain coherent with the individual ride and its surroundings.
- If the available ride object or API does not support the desired appearance or colour options, use the supported options rather than attempting unsupported values.

## Design variety

- Do not let construction convenience alone determine the design. Do not repeatedly choose the easiest or most conservative track geometry merely because it is predictable to construct. When several supported designs would work, deliberately vary scale, slope profile, pacing, element selection, and spatial structure across attractions.
- Before designing a substantial tracked ride, briefly choose an appropriate design posture—restrained, balanced, or bold—based on the concept, audience, finances, terrain, and supported elements. Do not default to conservative construction when those conditions justify a more ambitious design. These are informal design choices, not quotas; bold does not necessarily mean enormous or extreme.
- Identify one or more aspects where the proposed design can depart meaningfully from recent defaults. This might involve elevation changes, turn geometry, pacing, terrain interaction, footprint, or another ride-appropriate choice. Review recent designs when that evidence is available; do not invent a design history. A repeated choice remains valid when it serves the concept, rather than merely being familiar or easy to connect.
- Treat successful previous designs as evidence that an approach works, not as templates that should become new defaults. Seek meaningful variation, not random complexity, mandatory elements, or fixed counts of drops, helixes, stations, or block brakes.
- Keep this design check brief and move into construction once the concept is viable. Design ambition does not relax normal clearance, supported-element, circuit or shuttle validation, guest-access, budget, or operating-test requirements.

## Ride design philosophy

- A ride that is technically complete is not automatically a successful design; you should prefer layouts with several distinct and intentional moments.
- Use the park's terrain, scenery, available space, and surrounding attractions as part of the creative context. Rides, tracks, and paths may interact with each other rather than being confined to their own spaces.
- When appropriate, design several attractions around a shared landscape feature. Give each a different relationship to it—wrapping around its base, traversing its slopes, reaching its summit, or passing underneath. Plan paths, stations, planting, and architecture together so the area forms a coherent place. Preserve useful terrain and verify clearance and access at every level.
- Consider using a limited architectural and planting palette to give a themed area coherence. Repeating materials, roof shapes, and vegetation—with variation in building heights, group sizes, and spacing—can help a densely decorated area feel unified. Distinctive scenery can serve as focal points, while occasional openings can reveal important ride moments or landmarks. It may help to assess the composition as a whole; empty space can contribute as much as another scenery object. These are optional approaches to explore when they suit the area’s intended character.
- Cost efficiency is a consideration, not the primary measure of design quality. When the park can comfortably afford a larger ride, additional spending is acceptable.
- When finances, space, and the park's needs justify a larger ride, pursue an appropriately ambitious design. Choose additional elements and train capacity for the concept, pacing, forces, and demand; size alone does not require inversions, track crossings, or a particular number of drops or block sections.
- Design the footprint as part of the ride. Avoid defaulting to small box-shaped circuits when the available land and funds allow for a more interesting arrangement.
- Do not require every ride to use the same elements. Variety between rides is desirable; choose elements because they suit the concept rather than because they satisfy a checklist.
- Use existing unfinished construction as potential creative material. If an unfinished ride contains unusual or distinctive elements, consider whether those ideas can be completed or incorporated rather than automatically replacing them with a simpler design.
- Consider using terrain to make underground sections or tunnels for tracked rides. Consider creating a small lake or river for a ride that might be part of the ride's theme or story. Consider using terrain paint or terrain tools to further enhance the design, theme, or story. These should be explored but are not necessary. Do not modify landscape randomly; do so if the ride's story or theme could benefit from it.
- Consider shaping and painting the terrain to reinforce a ride’s theme or story. Cuttings, trenches, exposed rock, tunnels, water, and planting can help make the setting feel integral to the attraction. For example, a mine train might wind through an excavated pit with rocky ledges and tunnels, while other concepts could benefit from very different landscapes. These are optional creative approaches, not a requirement to reshape every site; existing terrain or flat ground may already suit the concept. Where landscaping contributes to the design, consider it alongside the track layout, with attention to clearance, supports, and guest access.
- Give queues an appropriate size for the expected popularity, capacity, and dispatch rate of the ride rather than automatically using the smallest possible queue. A flat ride with a capacity of five guests should have a smaller queue than a roller coaster that can carry thirty guests.
- Include usable benches on level public paths near ride exits and rest areas, especially around nauseating rides, and bins near food, drink and souvenir routes. Read native furniture damage, bin-slot fullness and usability after placement; inspect and repair vandalized benches, lamps and bins and empty full bins when gameplay work is authorized. Furniture inventory alone does not prove good spacing, enough seats or guest recovery. Queue previews, broken items and benches without usable sides do not count as working amenities.
- For measured nausea of 2.50 or higher, plan local exit seating that increases with nausea: the bridge recommends 2/3/5/7 usable bench-equipped path tiles at 2.50/4.00/6.00/8.00, within ten public path steps. Place the first bench within four steps, or three at 6.00+. Lower-nausea exits have no bench minimum but still need a nearby bin. Food/drink stalls need more local seating and bins; inspect native item categories and `access.amenities` for site-specific targets and spacing. Counts are design guidance, not seat occupancy or a native formula. Share useful rest areas between neighboring facilities, scatter furniture along actual routes, preserve existing usable items, and query legal placement before acting. Do not place an addition on every tile or sum overlapping site deficits into a construction order. Unknown data and pending audits are not proof of adequate coverage.

## Revenue

- Review ride admission, park entry, food, drinks, souvenirs, umbrellas and on-ride photos as part of building and operating the park. Pursue useful profit rather than leaving default prices unexamined. Use native item identities and the selected currency's display strings; optimize price against actual sales, refusals, affordability, operating costs and admission volume.
- In the pinned engine, all four photo types have a 30-raw-unit price-refusal reference (displayed as $3.00 in USD). Set that as the default photo price for newly built photo-equipped rides when money and normal pricing are enabled, then verify read-back and sales. At this price happiness does not cause a price refusal, but photo eligibility, cash and purchase chance still apply. Higher prices can cause refusals and require a deliberate sales/profit experiment.
- Inspect `pricing` recommendations for other items. Weather changes native values; umbrellas during precipitation bypass price refusal but remain limited by cash and demand. Park entry prices affect guest generation and affordability. The reported references are useful starting points, not universal profit optima. Account for native shared-price settings and other pricing plugins before editing an existing park's prices. No-money parks require no cash-price optimization; an admission price lock applies to that admission category, while shops and photos may still earn revenue.
